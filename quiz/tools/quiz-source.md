# The Eden Hypothesis: Chapter Quiz

2026-11-01 · @Dawn

Fifty multiple-choice questions cover the Introduction and all twenty-one chapters of the master manuscript of October 1, 2026, at least two per chapter. The answer and a short explanation follow each question. A question marked *(the book's reading)* tests the book's thesis; the others test facts the scholarly literature treats as settled, and an explanation notes any live dispute.

## Introduction: The Country Upstream of Everything

**1.** Jan Vansina's term for the stretch of oral tradition that thins out between the last few remembered generations and a people's origin stories is

-   A. the mythic threshold

-   B. the floating gap

-   C. the chronological horizon

-   D. the memory ceiling

**Answer: B.** Vansina found that oral histories keep recent generations in detail and their origins in myth, with a gap between that moves forward each generation. The Introduction's vividness gradient applies the pattern to Genesis.

**2.** In the "empty sanctuary" test, which retelling of Genesis gives Eden a sanctuary, has Abraham keep the Feast of Tabernacles, and gives Levi priestly robes and a tithe at Bethel?

-   A. the Genesis Apocryphon

-   B. Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities*

-   C. the Book of Jubilees

-   D. the Targum Onkelos

**Answer: C.** Jubilees, written in the second century BC, supplies the temple furniture that the patriarchal chapters of Genesis never mention. Appendix VII uses it as the control case: a text of known late date that fails the test.

## Part One, Chapters 1–6

### Chapter 1: Four Heads

**3.** English Bibles say the river of Eden "became four rivers" (Gen 2:10). The Hebrew word at the end of the verse literally means

-   A. branches

-   B. mouths

-   C. heads

-   D. streams

**Answer: C.** The word is *rāʾšîm*, the plural of *rōʾš*, "head." Read with the participle *yōṣēʾ*, "going out," the verse describes one water leaving a high country and parting, which points upstream rather than to the Persian Gulf, where the Tigris and Euphrates converge.

**4.** Genesis 2:14 says the Tigris runs *qidmat ʾaššûr*, "east of Assyria." Which setting fits the phrase best? *(the book's reading)*

-   A. the Neo-Assyrian empire, whose capitals lay at Nineveh and Kalhu

-   B. the Persian satrapy of Athura

-   C. the Old Assyrian city-state of Ashur, which stood on the west bank of the Tigris

-   D. the Seleucid province of Mesopotamia

**Answer: C.** Anyone standing in Ashur looked east across the river. The Neo-Assyrian capitals and most of the empire's heartland lay east of the Tigris, so for the empire the river ran through Assyria rather than past it.

**5.** The name Ararat (Gen 8:4) first appears in Assyrian inscriptions, as *Uruatri*, in the reign of

-   A. Shalmaneser I, in the thirteenth century BC

-   B. Sargon II, in the eighth century BC

-   C. Hammurabi, in the eighteenth century BC

-   D. Sennacherib, in the seventh century BC

**Answer: A.** *Uruatri* names a highland region of several "lands"; the kingdom of Urartu around Lake Van took shape only in the ninth century. The book treats the name as a later label laid over an older landscape, like the Chaldeans of Ur.

### Chapter 2: The First of Everything

**6.** The thousands of animal bones from the fill of the Göbekli Tepe enclosures come from

-   A. domestic sheep and goats

-   B. wild species, including gazelle, aurochs, boar, and birds

-   C. domestic cattle and pigs

-   D. mostly fish and waterfowl

**Answer: B.** Every identified species is wild (Peters and Schmidt 2004). The builders had no pottery, no metal, and no domestic livestock, yet raised monoliths weighing up to about ten tons.

**7.** Since Klaus Schmidt's death in 2014, the German Archaeological Institute team under Lee Clare has revised his "sanctuary without a settlement" model. Which finding drives the revision?

-   A. ordinary buildings, rock-cut cisterns, and thousands of grinding stones on the mound

-   B. radiocarbon dates placing the enclosures after 7000 BC

-   C. inscriptions identifying the central pillars as named gods

-   D. evidence that the site was a royal tomb

**Answer: A.** The new work points to people living on the ridge and processing wild cereals in bulk. The team also doubts Schmidt's view that the enclosures were deliberately buried, reading much of the fill as slope wash and collapse.

### Chapter 3: The Datable Garden

**8.** The oldest swords yet found, nine arsenical-copper blades from about 3300 BC, three of them inlaid with silver, came from

-   A. the Royal Cemetery at Ur

-   B. the princely tombs at Alaca Höyük

-   C. the palace at Arslantepe on the upper Euphrates

-   D. the cemetery at Varna on the Black Sea

**Answer: C.** The Italian mission under Alba Palmieri found them in the 1980s on the floor of a palace room at Arslantepe, near Malatya. The sword at the gate of Eden (Gen 3:24) therefore sets a floor for the telling no earlier than the late fourth millennium.

**9.** Critics long cited the iron of Tubal-cain (Gen 4:22) as proof of Iron Age composition. On what grounds does the chapter reverse that reading? *(the book's reading)*

-   A. *barzel* in Genesis means copper, not iron

-   B. Bronze Age iron was rare, prized, and mostly meteoritic, and Genesis treats it as one legendary smith's specialty rather than a commonplace

-   C. iron smelting is securely attested in Anatolia before 3000 BC

-   D. the verse is absent from the Septuagint

**Answer: B.** The Gerzeh beads (before 3200 BC) and Tutankhamun's dagger are meteoritic iron, and the Alaca Höyük dagger probably is, on a portable XRF reading of its corroded surface (Jambon 2017); Old Assyrian texts price iron above gold, if their metal *amūtum* is iron, as most Assyriologists hold. The Iron Age books of the Bible treat iron as ordinary; Genesis 4 treats it as a marvel, the register of the Bronze Age.

### Chapter 4: The Memory in the Names

**10.** The Mari archive divides the herding tribes of the middle Euphrates into the Binu Yamina and the Binu Sim'al. The names mean

-   A. the Sons of the River and the Sons of the Steppe

-   B. the Sons of the Tent and the Sons of the City

-   C. the Sons of the Moon and the Sons of the Sun

-   D. the Sons of the Right Hand and the Sons of the Left, that is, Southerners and Northerners

**Answer: D.** For someone facing the sunrise, the right hand is south and the left hand north. Among the towns in their range was Nahur on the upper Habur, the "city of Nahor" of Genesis 24:10.

**11.** In the first-fruits liturgy of Deuteronomy 26:5, the Israelite farmer declares that his father was a wandering

-   A. Amorite

-   B. Chaldean

-   C. Aramean

-   D. Hebrew

**Answer: C.** The tension drives the chapter: the Mari scribes call the patriarchs' kind of herders Amorites, the Bible usually names Amorites as enemies, and Israel's oldest creed says Aramean.

### Chapter 5: The Languages of Abraham

**12.** In 2022 Andrew George and Manfred Krebernik published two Old Babylonian tablets that changed the study of Amorite. What were they?

-   A. Amorite royal annals written in an early alphabet

-   B. Egyptian execration texts naming Amorite chiefs

-   C. bilingual vocabularies with Amorite phrases beside Akkadian translations

-   D. a Hebrew–Amorite treaty from Mari

**Answer: C.** Before them Amorite was known almost entirely from personal names. The phrase-book tablets show a coherent Northwest Semitic language recognizably related to Hebrew.

**13.** At the cairn of witness in Gilead (Gen 31:47), Laban and Jacob name the heap in two languages. Which pairing is correct?

-   A. Laban, *Galeed* in Hebrew; Jacob, *Jegar-sahadutha* in Aramaic

-   B. Laban, *Jegar-sahadutha* in Aramaic; Jacob, *Galeed* in Hebrew

-   C. both in Akkadian, with different spellings

-   D. Laban in Amorite; Jacob in Egyptian

**Answer: B.** The text records both names without comment. The chapter reads the verse as a snapshot of everyday multilingualism among herders moving between Aram and Canaan.

### Chapter 6: The Living Book

**14.** Genesis 14:14 has Abraham pursue the raiders "unto Dan," a city called Laish until the period of the Judges (Judg 18:29). How does the Agglomerative Hypothesis treat the name? *(the book's reading)*

-   A. as proof that the whole chapter was composed after the Judges

-   B. as a later update of an older text, made for readers who knew the place only as Dan

-   C. as a different city named Dan in Mesopotamia

-   D. as a copyist's error for Damascus

**Answer: B.** A gloss presupposes both an original and a later reader who needed it explained. The same logic covers Luz and Bethel (Gen 28:19; 35:6) and the Edomite king list of Genesis 36:31.

**15.** The formula *ʾēlleh tôlĕdôt*, "these are the generations of," heads eleven sections of Genesis, ten times in that form and once, at 5:1, as *zeh sēper tôlĕdôt*, "this is the book of the generations." What did P. J. Wiseman propose about it?

-   A. that it is a scribal colophon closing a tablet, as in cuneiform practice

-   B. that it marks the start of each new source document

-   C. that it was added by the Septuagint translators

-   D. that it counts generations in a fixed seventy-year unit

**Answer: A.** Wiseman's clay-archive mechanism remains a minority view. The chapter adds that the headings are generally assigned to the Priestly strand, which narrows the dispute to how old that strand is.

## Part One, Chapters 7–12

### Chapter 7: Two Stories, One World

**16.** In the Old Babylonian *Atrahasis*, what do the gods use to make humanity?

-   A. the tears of the sun god

-   B. the body of Tiamat, split in two

-   C. reeds woven by Enlil

-   D. clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god

**Answer: D.** Enki and the birth goddess Mami shape humans to take over the canal-digging labor of the rebellious Igigi gods. The epic dates to roughly 1700 BC.

**17.** What parallel does the chapter draw between *Atrahasis* and Genesis 1–2? *(the book's reading)*

-   A. both shift from a cosmic register to an intimate scene of a craftsman shaping a creature, inside a single composition

-   B. both tell of humanity's creation twice, in contradictory versions

-   C. both were recited on the fourth day of the Akitu festival

-   D. both name the same four rivers of paradise

**Answer: A.** No Assyriologist divides *Atrahasis* into an "Enlil source" and an "Enki source" on the strength of its change in register. A criterion that would split Genesis 1 from Genesis 2 on register alone would split *Atrahasis* the same way.

### Chapter 8: Before the Patriarchs

**18.** Which identification in the Table of Nations does the chapter treat as the single most secure, attested continuously in Sumerian, Akkadian, and Hebrew?

-   A. Tarshish

-   B. Gomer

-   C. Magog

-   D. the Amorites

**Answer: D.** Sumerian MAR.TU, Akkadian *Amurru*, and Hebrew *ʾĕmōrî* name the same people across more than a thousand years. The same population supplies the naming patterns of Abraham's family.

**19.** Gomer, Ashkenaz, and Madai sit at the edge of the Table's confidence gradient because

-   A. they name the Cimmerians, Scythians, and Medes, who enter the Assyrian record only between the ninth and seventh centuries BC, and some identifications rest on reconstruction

-   B. they are Egyptian names transcribed through Hurrian

-   C. they appear only in the Septuagint

-   D. they are Sumerian city-states that vanished before 2000 BC

**Answer: A.** Ashkenaz matches the Assyrian *Aškuza* only if a scribe once misread a *waw* as a *nun*. A list can be updated at its margins only if an older list stands at its center.

### Chapter 9: Fingerprints of a Vanished World

**20.** Eric Cline's symbolic date of 1177 BC for the Late Bronze Age collapse corresponds to

-   A. the sack of Babylon by the Hittites

-   B. the death of Tutankhamun

-   C. the fall of Mari to Hammurabi

-   D. the eighth year of Ramesses III, when Egyptian records describe a great movement of the Sea Peoples

**Answer: D.** The collapse ended the interconnected world of the Hittites, Ugarit, Mycenae, and Egyptian Canaan. Philistine settlement on the coast belongs to its aftermath.

**21.** Genesis 21 and 26 call Abimelech of Gerar a Philistine, a people not settled in Canaan before the collapse. How does the chapter resolve the difficulty? *(the book's reading)*

-   A. the ethnic label is a later update; the narrative beneath it portrays a Bronze Age city-state ruler

-   B. the Philistines arrived in Canaan in the third millennium BC

-   C. Abimelech is an Egyptian governor misidentified by the scribes

-   D. the episodes are late inventions and carry no older memory

**Answer: A.** Abimelech's court protocol and the disputes over wells fit the Bronze Age city-state world. A scribe who knew the coastal people as Philistines applied the familiar name to the inhabitants of an older story.

**22.** According to the chapter, what pattern unites the camels of the patriarchal narratives? *(the book's reading)*

-   A. they carry ordinary freight between Canaanite towns

-   B. they appear only in poetic passages

-   C. they arrive from outside Canaan, as a royal gift from Pharaoh or from Aram-naharaim and the north

-   D. they are always ridden in battle

**Answer: C.** The reading is possible, not proven: some references may be scribal updates, others memories of rare imports. Either way the camels show long transmission rather than late composition.

### Chapter 10: The Inherited Framework

**23.** Julius Wellhausen's *Prolegomena* (first published in 1878 as *Geschichte Israels*, vol. 1; retitled 1883) gave classic form to the Graf–Kuenen–Wellhausen thesis, often summed up in the Latin tag *lex post prophetas*. The phrase means

-   A. the prophets wrote the law

-   B. the law was lost until the prophets found it

-   C. law and prophecy arose together under Moses

-   D. the law came after the prophets, so the Priestly material is late

**Answer: D.** Wellhausen dated the Priestly law to the exile and after, and placed Genesis 1 at the far end of that development. The late date of Genesis rests more on that reconstruction of Israel's religion than on any dated manuscript. The tag is a later summary of the school's position; Wellhausen himself credits Graf with placing the law after the prophets.

**24.** Persian loanwords cluster in Esther, Daniel, Ezra, Nehemiah, and Chronicles. What word does Genesis use for Eden's garden?

-   A. *gan*, the ordinary Hebrew word, never the Persian loanword *pardēs*

-   B. *pardēs*, borrowed from Old Iranian

-   C. *paradeisos*, a Greek borrowing

-   D. *karmel*, "orchard"

**Answer: A.** The Septuagint translators chose *paradeisos*, which is how Eden became "paradise." The silence has limits: Ezekiel also calls Eden a *gan*, so the absence rules out an author steeped in Achaemenid administration but cannot by itself date the core.

### Chapter 11: Joseph's Book

**25.** On what grounds does the book set Moses aside as the author of Genesis?

-   A. Moses wrote only in Egyptian hieroglyphs

-   B. the Mishnah assigns Genesis to Ezra

-   C. Moses never appears in Genesis, and the book shows no knowledge of the law and wilderness that fill the four books after it

-   D. Genesis quotes Deuteronomy

**Answer: C.** The book proposes instead a man raised in the most literate court of the Bronze Age, with every reason to fix his family's memory before Egypt absorbed it: Joseph. The claim is a minority position and the book names it as such.

**26.** Joseph coaches his brothers to tell Pharaoh that they have been keepers of cattle "from our youth even until now" (Gen 46:34). What does the verse say this will secure?

-   A. settlement in Goshen, because every shepherd is an abomination to the Egyptians

-   B. posts as overseers of Pharaoh's royal herds

-   C. exemption from the famine tax

-   D. permission to return to Canaan

**Answer: A.** Pharaoh does also offer the brothers charge of his own livestock (47:6), but the verse names Goshen. The chapter reads the move as Joseph using Egypt's contempt for herders to keep his family distinct.

### Chapter 12: The Shape of the Story

**27.** The Joseph story runs the plot of the Middle Kingdom *Tale of Sinuhe* in reverse. Which contrast captures the reversal?

-   A. Sinuhe becomes a vizier; Joseph becomes a priest

-   B. Sinuhe marries an Egyptian; Joseph marries a Levantine woman

-   C. Sinuhe returns to Egypt in disgrace; Joseph returns to Canaan in triumph

-   D. Sinuhe longs for an Egyptian burial at home; Joseph, embalmed in Egypt, asks that his bones leave it

**Answer: D.** Sinuhe flees to the Levant, marries a chieftain's daughter, and dreads burial "wrapped in a sheepskin" among Asiatics. Joseph is carried to Egypt, marries a priest's daughter, and makes his kin swear to carry his bones out (Gen 50:25).

**28.** Read as seven poetic lines, Genesis 2:1–4a forms a ring. What stands alone at its center?

-   A. "Thus the heavens and the earth were finished"

-   B. "These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth"

-   C. "God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it"

-   D. "In the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens"

**Answer: C.** The seventh line reverses the order to "earth and heavens" and introduces the name Yahweh Elohim. Source critics read that join as a seam between documents; the chapter reads it as a deliberate signal that the story is about to invert. *(the book's reading)*

**29.** Redford dated the Joseph story late partly by names such as Potiphera, whose *pa-di-* type flourishes from the later New Kingdom onward. How does the chapter frame the dispute?

-   A. all the names are genuine Egyptian formations; the question is whether their surface spelling is original or refreshed in transmission

-   B. the names are Persian, so the story is post-exilic

-   C. the names are Akkadian, borrowed through Mari

-   D. the names were invented by the Septuagint translators

**Answer: A.** Kitchen points to older Middle Kingdom equivalents and to the modernizing of names in transmission. Chapter 10 concedes that the heavier numbers currently sit on Redford's side.

## Part Two, Chapters 13–17

### Chapter 13: The Man Who Answered Egypt

**30.** Which Egyptian creation theology has the creator make the world through the thought of his heart and the speech of his tongue?

-   A. the Ogdoad of Hermopolis

-   B. the Memphite Theology of Ptah, carved on the Shabaka Stone

-   C. the Ennead of Heliopolis

-   D. Khnum's potter's wheel at Elephantine

**Answer: B.** The Shabaka Stone dates to about 700 BC. The chapter treats it as a copy of far older texts; Egyptologists still dispute whether it preserves an older original or was composed in the Twenty-fifth Dynasty.

**31.** Which Middle Kingdom text calls humankind the creator's "cattle" and "his images who came forth from his body"?

-   A. the *Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor*

-   B. the *Admonitions of Ipuwer*

-   C. the *Book of the Dead*, spell 125

-   D. the *Instruction for Merikare*

**Answer: D.** Egypt had already extended the image beyond the king in this one text, while royal ideology kept it for Pharaoh. Genesis 1 gives the image to every human being, male and female, and strips away the pantheon around it.

### Chapter 14: The Priest-King's Garden

**32.** The man is set in the garden "to dress it and to keep it" (Gen 2:15), *ʿābad* and *šāmar*. Where else does the Torah pair these two verbs?

-   A. for farmers at harvest in Leviticus 23

-   B. for the king's duties in Deuteronomy 17

-   C. for the Levites' service at the tabernacle in Numbers 3:7–8

-   D. for shepherds in Genesis 30

**Answer: C.** The pairing makes the gardener a priest as well as a royal steward (Wenham, "Sanctuary Symbolism"). The chapter reads Eden as a royal garden that was also a sanctuary, a distinction the Bronze Age had not yet drawn. *(the book's reading)*

**33.** God is heard "walking" in the garden in the cool of the day (Gen 3:8). Which later use of the same verb form does the chapter set beside it?

-   A. for the divine presence moving through Israel's camp and tabernacle (Lev 26:12; Deut 23:14; 2 Sam 7:6–7)

-   B. for Abraham's journey from Harran

-   C. for the serpent's motion after the curse

-   D. for Jacob limping away from the Jabbok

**Answer: A.** The hithpael *mithallēk* links the garden with later sacred space, as do the cherubim at the gate and the river that waters the world. Ezekiel calls Eden "the garden of God" on "the holy mountain of God" (Ezek 28:13–14).

### Chapter 15: The Priest-King of Salem

**34.** A Hittite myth of the Late Bronze Age names the West Semitic high god *Elkunirsa*. The name renders

-   A. *El ʿOlam*, "El the Everlasting"

-   B. *El Shaddai*, "El of the Mountain"

-   C. *Ilu-ab*, "El the Father"

-   D. *El qōnê ʾarṣi*, "El, creator of earth"

**Answer: D.** The title matches Melchizedek's *qōnê šāmayim wāʾāreṣ*, "maker of heaven and earth" (Gen 14:19). It shows the epithet was already old in the second millennium.

**35.** What does Abraham do with Melchizedek's title in his oath to the king of Sodom (Gen 14:22)?

-   A. he rejects El Elyon as a foreign god

-   B. he swears by "the LORD, the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth," binding the name Yahweh to the title

-   C. he renames El Elyon as Baal

-   D. he swears by the household gods of Terah

**Answer: B.** The chapter reads the oath as an identification, not a correction. A textual caution applies: the Septuagint, the Samaritan Pentateuch, and the Genesis Apocryphon lack "the LORD" in this verse, so the explicit equation may itself be a later layer.

### Chapter 16: The God

**36.** In *Atrahasis*, humans live by the blood of a slain god. What animates the human in Genesis 2:7?

-   A. the blood of the serpent

-   B. water from the four rivers

-   C. the breath of God blown into the nostrils, with no divine blood at all

-   D. a spark from the flaming sword

**Answer: C.** The clay is only clay, and the life is God's breath, *nišmat ḥayyîm*. The purpose inverts as well: humanity in *Atrahasis* feeds the gods, while the God of Genesis plants a garden to feed the human.

**37.** How does the book read the shift from *Elohim* in Genesis 1 to the compound *Yahweh Elohim* in Genesis 2–3? *(the book's reading)*

-   A. as proof that two separate authors wrote the chapters centuries apart

-   B. as a deliberate weld that identifies the transcendent creator with the intimate God who shapes and walks

-   C. as a scribal error later corrected

-   D. as a sign that the garden story is Babylonian

**Answer: B.** The compound appears nowhere in Genesis 1. Documentary critics treat it as a source marker; the book reads it as a theological claim folded into a name.

### Chapter 17: The Man

**38.** In the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, the goddess who pinches off clay to make Enkidu in the steppe is

-   A. Ishtar

-   B. Ninsun

-   C. Ereshkigal

-   D. Aruru

**Answer: D.** Enkidu, like *ʾādām* from *ʾădāmâ*, comes from the earth. He is made as a match for a restless king and tamed into city life; Adam is made in the divine image, a title the region's royal ideologies reserved above all for kings.

**39.** Art historians distinguish three types of the Master of Animals: heraldic, combat, and enthroned. What does Genesis 2 substitute for all three?

-   A. a hunt with bow and spear

-   B. a sacrifice of the first animals

-   C. naming: God brings the animals and the man calls each one

-   D. a covenant sealed with animal blood

**Answer: C.** Dominion in the garden is exercised by word, not by grip, combat, or rank. The naming also shows the man that no creature yet corresponds to him.

## Part Two, Chapters 18–21

### Chapter 18: The Woman

**40.** The man is *formed* (*yāṣar*), the potter's verb. Which verb does Genesis 2:22 use for the making of the woman?

-   A. *bānâ*, "to build," the verb for raising a house, a city, or a sanctuary

-   B. *bārāʾ*, "to create"

-   C. *ʿāśâ*, "to make"

-   D. *yāṣar* again, as for the man

**Answer: A.** The word *ṣēlāʿ*, usually rendered "rib," elsewhere names the side of a building, the side of the ark, and the side chambers of the temple. She is the only creature made from living human substance and the only one made inside the garden.

**41.** Samuel Noah Kramer connected the rib of Genesis 2 with Nin-ti in the Sumerian myth *Enki and Ninhursag*, read as both "Lady of the Rib" and "Lady Who Makes Live." What caution does the chapter attach?

-   A. the myth survives only in Greek translation

-   B. Nin-ti is a male deity

-   C. the myth dates to the Persian period

-   D. the reading is Kramer's and has been questioned: the myth never spells out the pun, and the Sumerian verb "to live" is properly *til*, a near-homophone of *ti*

**Answer: D.** If Kramer is right, Genesis keeps the meaning of a pun whose linguistic key was lost in Hebrew. If he is wrong, the Hebrew story still stands on its own verbs.

**42.** The woman is called *ʿēzer kĕnegdô*, "a help meet for him." Elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, *ʿēzer* most often describes

-   A. a hired servant

-   B. God helping Israel

-   C. a younger sibling

-   D. a military auxiliary of a foreign king

**Answer: B.** See, for example, Exodus 18:4, Deuteronomy 33:7, and Psalm 121:1–2. The word carries no sense of subordination, and *kĕnegdô* means "corresponding to him" or "opposite him."

### Chapter 19: The Tree

**43.** In Tablet XI of the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, how does Gilgamesh lose the plant that makes the old young again?

-   A. Utnapishtim takes it back

-   B. it withers on the voyage home

-   C. a serpent eats it while he bathes in a pool, then sheds its skin

-   D. Ishtar burns it in anger

**Answer: C.** The reaching human, the plant of life, and the serpent that takes it away are all older than Genesis. In Eden, by contrast, the tree of life stands open the whole time: a gift within reach, not a prize hunted at the ends of the earth.

**44.** On the leaves of which sacred Egyptian tree did the gods write a king's allotted years?

-   A. the sycamore of Hathor

-   B. the date palm of Heliopolis

-   C. the *kiškanu* of Eridu

-   D. the *ished* tree

**Answer: D.** The *kiškanu* is Mesopotamian, and the sycamore of the tree goddess offered bread and water to the dead. The tree of the knowledge of good and evil, by contrast, has no real parallel in the older literature.

### Chapter 20: The Snake

**45.** The serpent is *ʿārûm*, "subtil" or "shrewd" (Gen 3:1). On which word in the verse just before it, Genesis 2:25, does it play?

-   A. *ʿāpār*, "dust"

-   B. *ʿărûmmîm*, "naked," said of the man and the woman

-   C. *ʿēden*, "delight"

-   D. *ʿēṣ*, "tree"

**Answer: B.** The couple are naked and unashamed; the creature beside them is shrewd. The pun binds the scene of innocence to the scene of temptation.

**46.** Hebrew *nāḥāš*, "serpent," shares its root with the verb *niḥēš*, "to practice divination." Where else in Genesis does that verb appear?

-   A. in Laban's divination (30:27) and in Joseph's silver cup (44:5, 15)

-   B. in the flood story's raven and dove

-   C. in the blessing of Judah (49:10)

-   D. in Abraham's dream at the covenant of the pieces

**Answer: A.** The chapter reads the cup as a quiet authorial echo from a writer at home in the Delta, where the cobra crowned the king and Apophis threatened the sunrise. *(the book's reading)*

**47.** Does the Hebrew text of Genesis 3 identify the serpent with Satan?

-   A. yes, in 3:1

-   B. yes, in the curse of 3:14

-   C. no; the identification arises later, in texts such as Wisdom of Solomon 2:24 and Revelation 12:9

-   D. no; the Septuagint adds the name, but the Hebrew lacks it

**Answer: C.** Genesis calls the serpent a creature "which the LORD God had made," shrewder than any beast of the field. It demotes a figure that stood elsewhere for chaos, healing, and royal protection.

### Chapter 21: The Guardians

**48.** After the cherubim take their post at Genesis 3:24, the word *kĕrûb* next appears in the Torah where?

-   A. Jacob's ladder at Bethel (Gen 28)

-   B. the burning bush (Exod 3)

-   C. the Song of the Sea (Exod 15)

-   D. the mercy seat of the tabernacle (Exod 25:18)

**Answer: D.** Cherubim appear where sanctuaries appear. Their absence across the whole patriarchal narrative, with its open-air altars and standing stones, is the chapter's "templeless book" test. *(the book's reading)*

**49.** The man was placed in the garden "to keep it" (2:15). The cherubim are posted "to keep the way of the tree of life" (3:24). The shared verb is

-   A. *ʿābad*, "to serve"

-   B. *šāmar*, "to keep, guard"

-   C. *gāraš*, "to drive out"

-   D. *nāṭaʿ*, "to plant"

**Answer: B.** The chapter reads the posting as the reassignment of a vacated office rather than a punishment. The tree of life is guarded, not destroyed.

**50.** Hebrew *kĕrûb* is very probably related to which Akkadian term?

-   A. *kāribu*, "one who blesses," an intercessor figure at the threshold

-   B. *karābu*, "to curse"

-   C. *lamassu*, the winged bull

-   D. *kerbu*, "field"

**Answer: A.** The verb *karābu* means "to bless, pray, greet," not "to curse." The etymology is widely accepted but not certain; *lamassu* names a different guardian type.
